# Proposal: The Marriage Bar

**Subject:** The Marriage Bar — marriage can be desired and pair-specific commitment can be present while a socially defined economic-readiness threshold remains uncleared. Cohabitation and marriage therefore need not be successive labels on one automatic ladder.

**Suggested parent entry:** `frameworks:readiness-gate` as a substantive sub-entry. The Readiness Gate already separates interest from present capacity; this proposal names and evidences the economic threshold specific to marriage. Cross-link `statistics:stat-married-cohabiting`, `statistics:stat-cohabitation-outcomes`, and `statistics:stat-cohab-timing` without merging their distinct questions.

## Proposed claims and evidence tiers

1. **Wanting marriage and feeling economically ready for marriage are separate states.** Among U.S. non-engaged cohabiters who said they wanted to marry someday, two-thirds cited their own or their partner's financial unreadiness as at least a minor reason they were not engaged or married; 21% called insufficient career progress a major reason. **Suggested tier: Tier 2** for a weighted probability-panel description of stated reasons, with self-report and cross-sectional limits.  
   Primary source: [Pew Research Center, *Why people get married or move in with a partner* (2019)](https://www.pewresearch.org/social-trends/2019/11/06/why-people-get-married-or-move-in-with-a-partner/).

2. **The economic threshold is partly a symbolic milestone standard, rather than only the immediate arithmetic of sharing a home.** In 115 in-depth interviews with working- and lower-middle-class cohabiters, respondents commonly described marriage as appropriate after their finances had changed; cohabitation could already be workable while marriage remained gated by savings, debt reduction, stable employment, or the ability to stage an acceptable wedding. **Suggested tier: Tier 3** qualitative mechanism evidence.  
   Primary source: [Smock, Manning & Porter, “Everything's There Except Money” (2005)](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1741-3737.2005.00162.x).

3. **Observed transitions support a couple-level economic bar, while undercutting an exclusively male-provider explanation.** Prospective monthly SIPP panels from 1996–2013 found that wealth and couple earnings relative to an empirically defined marriage standard strongly predicted transition from cohabitation to marriage after adjustment for absolute earnings; higher and more equal earnings also predicted lower separation risk. **Suggested tier: Tier 2** longitudinal observational evidence; association rather than randomized causation.  
   Primary source: [Ishizuka, *The Economic Foundations of Cohabiting Couples' Union Transitions* (2018)](https://doi.org/10.1007/s13524-018-0651-1).

4. **A favorable earnings shock does not mechanically restore marriage when the cultural/institutional channel differs.** The fracking boom raised earnings for non-college men and increased births, but the study found no associated increase in marriage, unlike the earlier Appalachian coal boom comparison. **Suggested tier: Tier 2** quasi-experimental evidence and a boundary on income-only stories.  
   Primary source: [Kearney & Wilson, *Male Earnings, Marriageable Men, and Nonmarital Fertility* (2018)](https://doi.org/10.1162/rest_a_00739); [open working paper](https://www.nber.org/papers/w23408).

## Candidate aliases

- marriage bar
- economic bar to marriage
- financial readiness threshold
- marital milestone standard
- economic marriage threshold

## Candidate common misreadings

- Living with a partner means the relationship will naturally become marriage once love is strong.
- Couples delay marriage because they want expensive weddings, while ordinary finances play little role.
- An unemployed man is automatically unready for marriage regardless of his partner's resources.

## Boundary conditions

- The Pew and SIPP findings are U.S.-specific and describe cohorts/institutions of their periods; the level and contents of the bar can vary by class, culture, race, age, prior marriage, parenthood, housing market, and legal regime.
- Stated reasons can rationalize decisions after the fact. The Pew survey identifies reported barriers, while the SIPP analysis supplies prospective behavioral association; neither randomly assigns financial readiness.
- “Economic readiness” can mean stable earnings, assets, debt reduction, housing, wedding affordability, or a comparison standard. These are empirically separable and should not be collapsed into income alone.
- The bar belongs to the couple and its context. Evidence does not justify treating only men's earnings as causally decisive or requiring one breadwinner arrangement.
- Remaining below a perceived milestone can delay marriage without showing low love, low commitment, or poor relationship quality. Clearing it also does not guarantee marriage or durability.

## Deliberately not claimed

- Cohabitation causes delayed marriage, divorce, or lower relationship quality.
- Every couple should clear a fixed income, savings, homeownership, or career threshold before marrying.
- Expensive weddings are necessary, desirable, or the principal reason couples delay.
- Financial hardship is the sole cause of marriage decline or nonmarital childbearing.
- Male earnings alone determine whether a couple marries.
- Marriage is superior to cohabitation, or every cohabiting relationship is intended to become marriage.
- Meeting the economic bar causes marriage; the core evidence is descriptive, qualitative, longitudinal-observational, and context-dependent.
