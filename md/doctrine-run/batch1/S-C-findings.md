# C6 Findings — Mate-value discrepancy, relationship satisfaction/infidelity, and the alternatives-visibility conditional

Claim under test: "Being higher mate-value than one's partner degrades relationship satisfaction and raises infidelity intent — mainly when attractive alternatives are visibly available." Split strictly per assignment into (a) the main effect and (b) the conditional (does alternatives-visibility moderate it).

## (a) MAIN EFFECT: relative mate value / discrepancy → satisfaction (and, separately, infidelity)

**Claim A1.** Partner-potential mate value discrepancy (how much better other people's actual partners fulfill a person's stated preferences than that person's own partner does) is a negative main-effect predictor of relationship satisfaction.
- Advanced by: Conroy-Beam, Goetz & Buss (2016), *Evolution and Human Behavior*.
- Evidence: Study 1, N=259 (MTurk, heterosexual, ongoing long-term relationships), forced-entry regression, beta = -.53, p<.001; zero-order r = -.50 with satisfaction. Study 2, N=300, zero-order r = -.39. Study 3, N=301 (methodologically improved, identical rating scales for self/partner/ideal), same pattern retained inside the interaction term (see b1 below).
- Design: all three studies cross-sectional, single-timepoint online survey.
- Tier: TIER 2. Three internal replications, but all by the same three authors, same recruitment pipeline ("identical posting" on MTurk across studies), same lab, no independent replication by an unaffiliated group.

**Claim A2.** Partner-self mate value discrepancy (partner rated higher in mate value than self) is a positive main-effect predictor of satisfaction, on its own.
- Advanced by: Conroy-Beam, Goetz & Buss (2016).
- Evidence: Study 1, beta = .11, p = .05 (marginal). This main effect is weak/marginal and is superseded by the interaction term reported under (b) - the authors' own stepwise model selection dropped preference fulfillment and kept only the discrepancy interaction, meaning the field's best-supported effect from this paper is not this simple main effect but the interaction in (b).
- Tier: TIER 2, marginal significance, single program.

**Claim A3 (prior/competing hypothesis, not independently verified here).** A general "ideal-standards mismatch" between partner traits and one's ideal preferences (not specifically mate-value discrepancy) reduces satisfaction. Advanced by Fletcher, Simpson, Thomas & Giles (1999); described by Conroy-Beam et al. as receiving "some support" from Meltzer, McNulty, Jackson & Karney (2014). I did not fetch either source directly - GAP, reached only secondhand via Conroy-Beam et al.'s literature review. Tier: TIER 3 as reported here (unverified secondhand characterization).

**Claim A4.** Attention to/awareness of alternatives (not mate-value discrepancy per se) correlates negatively with satisfaction, investment, and commitment, cross-sectionally.
- Advanced by: Rowland S. Miller (1997, *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 73(4), 758-766), reprised in Miller's 2007 Sydney Symposium draft chapter (raw-02.txt).
- Evidence: r = -.58 with satisfaction, r = -.52 with investment, r = -.61 with commitment, r = -.59 with love, r = -.39 with liking (original 1997 study; exact N for this specific sub-sample not stated in the chapter text I could access - UNVERIFIED N, chapter did not report it).
- Longitudinal extension (Simeon & Miller, 2005, unpublished conference paper): 3-month cross-lagged panel, N=167, satisfaction(T1) to attentiveness(T2) r = -.47; attentiveness(T1) to satisfaction(T2) r = -.38 - i.e., dissatisfaction predicts later attentiveness AND attentiveness predicts later erosion of satisfaction (bidirectional).
- Tier: the core 1997 finding is TIER 2 (single peer-reviewed study, JPSP, not independently replicated by an outside lab). The 3-month cross-lag figure is TIER 3 as sourced here - it comes from an unpublished conference presentation (Simeon & Miller, 2005), reported secondhand inside Miller's own later chapter, not verified against a peer-reviewed version.

**Claim A5 (infidelity, own mate value).** A person's overall mate value negatively predicts their own intention to commit infidelity; four mate-value sub-components (agreeableness/commitment, surgency, emotional stability, physical attractiveness) each uniquely predict infidelity intention.
- Source: a paper titled "Mate value both positively and negatively predicts intentions to commit an infidelity" (ScienceDirect record S0191886916308686, journal inferred from ISSN prefix to be *Personality and Individual Differences* but not confirmed). WebFetch returned HTTP 403/402 on both ResearchGate and ScienceDirect. GAP - reached only via search-engine paraphrase, no author names, year, or N recovered. Tier: TIER 3 (unverified secondhand). Note this claim is about a person's own absolute mate value, not the relative discrepancy vs. partner, and the snippet gives no indication an alternatives-visibility moderator was tested.

**Claim A6 (infidelity, gendered).** Relationship dissatisfaction is one of the most powerful predictors of women's actual infidelity but not men's infidelity.
- Advanced by: Buss, Goetz, Duntley, Asao & Conroy-Beam (2017), "The mate switching hypothesis," *Personality and Individual Differences* 104:143-149 (read in full but not saved as a raw file - see Concentration section below), citing Glass & Wright (1985, 1992) as the original data source. I did not fetch Glass & Wright directly - GAP, secondhand via the Buss et al. review. Tier: TIER 3 as sourced here.

## (b) THE CONDITIONAL: does the visibility/availability of alternatives moderate the discrepancy-to-satisfaction link?

**Claim B1 - the interaction test itself (the assignment's highest-value target).** Relative mate value discrepancy and the availability of better alternatives interact: relationship satisfaction declines with increasing alternative availability, but only for people who are themselves higher in mate value than their partner. People whose partner is higher in mate value than themselves stay satisfied regardless of how good the available alternatives are.
- Advanced by: Conroy-Beam, Goetz & Buss (2016), full text in raw-01.txt.
- Evidence (three internal studies, all cross-sectional, MTurk, US-based, heterosexual-relationship samples):
  - Study 1, N=259: interaction beta = .21, p<.001; full model R2 = 31.34%.
  - Study 2, N=300: interaction beta = .28, p<.001; path model shows the interaction mediates through satisfaction to increased mate-retention behavior (indirect path p = .01) and through trust to decreased mate-retention behavior (indirect path p = .001) - i.e., satisfaction and trust have opposite behavioral consequences.
  - Study 3, N=301 (same-scale replication addressing a measurement-scale confound in Studies 1-2): interaction beta = -.20, p<.001 (sign differs from Studies 1-2 due to different model-coding conventions between the forced-entry vs. stepwise specifications; the plotted pattern the authors describe is directionally the same as Studies 1-2 - higher-MV-than-partner people are satisfied regardless of alternatives, lower-MV people are satisfied only when alternatives are scarce).
  - No study in this set measured actual infidelity or infidelity intent - the dependent variables are relationship satisfaction (QMI) and mate-retention effort, not infidelity.
- Design: cross-sectional throughout, single timepoint, self-report survey. No longitudinal or behavioral-outcome data in this paper.
- Tier: TIER 2. Internally replicated three times but by the identical three authors, identical recruitment text, identical lab, zero independent replication.
- CRITICAL CONSTRUCT MISMATCH: the "alternatives" term here is not a measure of subjective visibility/awareness of alternatives. It is an objective, mathematically-computed statistic - the proportion of other survey respondents' actual partners (people the focal participant never met or saw) who would fulfill the focal participant's stated preferences better than their own partner does. The authors themselves flag this as a limitation: "the participants in our samples were drawn from a national population and are virtually guaranteed to not know one another." This paper tests discrepancy times statistical availability of better mates in a hypothetical pool, not discrepancy times perceived/visible alternatives. It is the closest thing anyone has run to the assignment's target interaction, but it does not literally test the "visibility" qualifier as worded.

**Claim B2 - concentration/citation-recycling finding (the assignment's load-bearing control).** Buss, Goetz, Duntley, Asao & Conroy-Beam (2017), "The mate switching hypothesis" (*Personality and Individual Differences*), states as established fact: "Individuals show decreased relationship satisfaction when they perceive they are higher in mate value than their romantic partner, but only if there exist highly desirable alternatives in the mating pool (Conroy-Beam et al., in press)." This is presented in a review/theory paper as supporting evidence for the mate-switching hypothesis. This citation is to the identical dataset as Claim B1 - "Conroy-Beam et al., in press" is the same Conroy-Beam, Goetz & Buss (2016) EHB paper. Author overlap: Conroy-Beam and Goetz appear on both papers; Buss is senior/corresponding author on both. Both papers are products of the same UT Austin Buss lab (Goetz completed her PhD there and remains a co-author with her former advisor). The two papers were in review/press within the same ~12-month window (empirical paper: received June 2015, revised April 2016; review paper: received Feb 2016, accepted July 2016) - this is near-simultaneous same-lab output, not independent theory validated against independent data. The entire evidentiary basis for the "mate switching hypothesis" review's claim about this specific conditional is the single Conroy-Beam et al. (2016) paper. No other empirical citation for this specific claim appears anywhere in the review paper I read in full. This is exactly the concentration risk the assignment's control flagged in advance: what looks like "the literature supporting X" in a review context is one dataset, cited twice, by overlapping authors.

**Claim B3 - the Rusbult/Miller-tradition analog (different vocabulary, different mechanism, genuinely independent lineage).** Attention to alternatives mediates the effect of alternative quality on subsequent commitment: alternative quality alone has a weaker direct effect on commitment; the erosion is stronger when the alternatives are actually attended to (i.e., psychologically "visible").
- Advanced by: Rowland S. Miller, citing Simeon & Miller (2005), N=167, Sobel's test t(167) = -7.58, p<.001, partial mediation.
- This is conceptually the closest existing analog to the assignment's "only when alternatives are visibly available" qualifier - visibility/attention is the literal mediating/moderating mechanism tested - but it is (i) about commitment, not satisfaction or infidelity intent as the outcome, (ii) not framed in mate-value-discrepancy terms at all (the investment-model tradition doesn't use "mate value discrepancy" as a construct - see terminology note below), and (iii) sourced from an unpublished conference paper for the mediation statistic specifically.
- Tier: TIER 2/3 - mediation figure is TIER 3 (unpublished, secondhand via Miller's own later chapter); the broader attentiveness-erodes-relationships pattern it sits inside is TIER 2 (Miller 1997, peer-reviewed, single lab).

**Claim B4 - Johnson & Rusbult (1989) devaluation-under-threat finding.** More-committed people devalue attractive alternative partners more strongly, and this devaluation is greatest specifically under high-threat conditions - i.e., when personally evaluating an extremely attractive (highly "visible"/salient) alternative in a live computer-dating paradigm (their Study 2). This is arguably an inverse-direction conditional: rather than "visible alternatives degrade satisfaction," Johnson & Rusbult show "commitment causes people to perceptually neutralize visible alternatives," a self-protective feedback loop that would work against, not for, the assignment's claim.
- I could not fetch Johnson & Rusbult (1989) directly - ResearchGate and the Northwestern faculty PDF mirror were not fetched in full; GAP, reached only via a WebSearch synthesis and via Miller's secondhand citations of it. Sample sizes and exact statistics for the three sub-studies (longitudinal, computer-dating, simulation) are UNVERIFIED.
- Tier: TIER 3 as sourced in this scout pass (secondhand); the underlying paper is a well-cited, peer-reviewed JPSP article and would likely earn TIER 2 on primary read, but I did not verify it primary.

**Claim B5 - infidelity-specific conditional, parallel-mediator (not interaction) design.** Perceived online-dating success predicts both perceived availability of alternative partners and mate-value discrepancy, which each separately predict attention to alternatives, which in turn predicts online-infidelity-related behavior (serial mediation), N=338, cross-sectional online-dating-app sample.
- Source: "Pathways to online infidelity: the roles of perceived online dating success, perceived availability of alternative partners, and mate value discrepancy" (ResearchGate ID 374848897). WebFetch returned HTTP 403. GAP - could not confirm authors, journal, year, or whether the two paths (availability-of-alternatives; mate-value-discrepancy) were tested as an interaction (multiplicative) term or merely as two parallel/independent mediators feeding the same downstream variable. From the search-engine paraphrase alone, this reads as a parallel-mediator model, not a moderation/interaction model. Tier: TIER 3 as sourced (unverified secondhand); flagged because it is the only source I located that puts "mate value discrepancy" and "alternative availability" in the same statistical model with an infidelity-adjacent outcome, and it deserves a primary re-read by whoever has ScienceDirect/ResearchGate access.

**Does a true discrepancy times alternatives-visibility interaction exist, tested with infidelity or infidelity intent as the outcome?** Not found. Claim B1 tests the interaction but with satisfaction/mate-retention-effort as outcomes, not infidelity. Claim B5 puts both predictors in one model with an infidelity-adjacent outcome but appears (unconfirmed) to use parallel mediation rather than a multiplicative interaction term. I found no study that tests (relative mate value discrepancy) times (visibility/availability of alternatives) predicting (infidelity or infidelity intent) as a formal interaction. If such a study exists, it was not surfaced by this search pass - treat its absence here as inconclusive, not as proof of non-existence, but this is the honest state of what was reachable.

## Terminology across the two traditions

The evolutionary-psychology/mate-switching tradition (Buss, Goetz, Conroy-Beam, Duntley, Asao) frames the phenomenon as "mate value discrepancy" (self vs. partner) interacting with "partner-potential mate value discrepancy" (an objective, computed measure of how good the available mating pool is). The investment-model/interdependence tradition (Thibaut & Kelley to Rusbult to Johnson & Rusbult to Miller) frames functionally adjacent territory as "comparison level for alternatives" (CLalt), "quality of alternatives" (a formal term in Rusbult's Investment Model equation alongside satisfaction and investment size), and "attention to alternatives" (Miller's own construct, operationalized as awareness/interest/distraction, not objective mate value). Neither tradition's core construct is a perfect match for the assignment's "visibility of alternatives" - the evolutionary-psych side has an objective pool-availability variable but not a subjective visibility variable; the investment-model side has a subjective attention/visibility variable (Miller) and a subjective perceived-quality variable (Rusbult's QoA) but does not frame the moderator in terms of the chooser's own relative mate value the way the evolutionary-psych side does. The two traditions are talking about adjacent-but-not-identical constructs with different vocabularies, and I did not find a paper that unifies them.

## Research-program concentration analysis

Evolutionary-psychology/mate-switching side is highly concentrated. All three "replications" of the core interaction (Studies 1-3 in Conroy-Beam, Goetz & Buss 2016) are the same three authors, same lab, same MTurk recruitment posting, run within roughly the same 1-2 year period. The one review paper (Buss, Goetz, Duntley, Asao & Conroy-Beam 2017) that treats this interaction as established evidence for the broader "mate switching hypothesis" shares 3 of 5 authors with the empirical paper and was written/reviewed in the same window - it is not an independent theoretical validation, it is the same lab restating its own recent finding as support for its own new theoretical framework. I found zero citations of the Conroy-Beam et al. (2016) interaction finding by any research group outside the Buss/Conroy-Beam/Goetz orbit in this search pass (this is not proof none exist - see Confidence Notes).

Investment-model/Rusbult side is comparatively decentralized, but only for the general quality-of-alternatives to commitment main effect, not for anything resembling the specific interaction under test. Per search-engine synthesis of Tran et al. (2019, *Personal Relationships*, "Commitment in relationships: An updated meta-analysis of the Investment Model" - GAP, paywalled, HTTP 402 on Wiley, not fetched primary), 202 independent samples and 50,427 participants across ~35 years (1980-2016) were meta-analyzed, with aggregate r = -.43 between quality of alternatives and commitment. That is a genuinely broad, multi-lab evidence base - but it is evidence for a simple, unmoderated main effect of perceived alternative quality on commitment, not for any interaction with relative mate value, and not with satisfaction or infidelity as the outcome. The specific chain that resembles the assignment's conditional - attention/visibility of alternatives moderating the alternatives-to-commitment link - traces back almost entirely to Rowland Miller's own single-author program (Miller 1997; Miller 2002 and Simeon & Miller 2005, both unpublished conference presentations from Miller's own lab with his own student). Johnson & Rusbult (1989) is a genuinely separate group (Rusbult was at University of Kentucky at the time) and is the one clearly independent data point in this tradition, but I did not verify it primary.

Net assessment on the control question: the evolutionary-psychology literature on this specific claim is a single research program citing itself; the investment-model literature has broad independent support for its own general "alternatives affect commitment" claim but almost no independent-lab work on the specific visibility/attention-as-moderator angle beyond one investigator's own program. Neither tradition offers an independently-replicated test of the assignment's precise conditional claim.

For the maintainer, not a proposed Lab change: if the LE Lab's canon ever encodes "higher mate value than partner hurts satisfaction," it should encode the FULL conditional (interaction with alternative availability), not the main effect alone - the source paper (raw-01.txt) explicitly found that people mated to higher-mate-value partners stay satisfied regardless of alternatives, and people mated to lower-mate-value partners are ALSO satisfied as long as alternatives are scarce. Collapsing this to "higher mate value than partner leads to dissatisfaction" without the alternatives qualifier would misstate the one study that actually tested it.
