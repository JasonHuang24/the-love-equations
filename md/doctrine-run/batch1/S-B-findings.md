## (a) Desire / frequency / satisfaction decline with relationship duration

**Statement:** Sexual desire, sexual frequency, and/or sexual satisfaction fall as relationship or
marital duration increases.

### McNulty, Wenner, & Fisher (2016) — PRIMARY, VERIFIED (raw-02.txt)
*Longitudinal Associations among Relationship Satisfaction, Sexual Satisfaction, and Frequency of
Sex in Early Marriage.* Archives of Sexual Behavior 45(1):85-97.
- Advanced by: McNulty and colleagues (Florida State University).
- Design: TWO PROSPECTIVE LONGITUDINAL STUDIES of newlywed couples, 8 waves of marital and sexual
  satisfaction and 7 waves of sexual frequency, at 6-8 month intervals, spanning ~4-4.5 years.
  Study 1: N=72 couples (north-central Ohio). Study 2: N=135 couples (eastern Tennessee). Combined
  N=207 couples (both convenience samples, "neither representative of the community from which the
  couples were drawn").
- Result: all three variables — marital satisfaction, sexual satisfaction, frequency of sex —
  "declined significantly over time." Exact growth-curve estimates (HLM, df=206): marital
  satisfaction Time effect b=-0.44, p<.001, effect size r=.48; sexual satisfaction Time effect
  b=-1.27, p<.001, r=.36; frequency of sex Time effect b=-3.96, p<.001, r=.29. In all three, a
  significant positive Time×Time interaction (marital b=0.15, r=.43; sexual satisfaction b=0.43,
  r=.35; frequency b=0.83, r=.18) means the decline is steepest early in marriage and flattens
  thereafter — a decelerating, not linear, decay curve.
- Can the design speak to mechanism? This IS a true within-person prospective design (unlike most
  of the literature below), so it can and does speak to genuine within-couple change over time, not
  merely cross-sectional duration differences between different couples. It cannot, however, speak
  to WHY (no security/familiarity measure is in the model); frequency-of-sex, sexual satisfaction,
  and marital satisfaction were shown to be reciprocally, bidirectionally linked to one another
  over time, but no attachment-security or familiarity construct was tested as a mechanism.
- Tier: **TIER 1-adjacent** — genuinely prospective, multi-wave, growth-curve modeled, controls
  attrition (empirical Bayes) and neuroticism; not independently replicated across an
  outside-lab dataset within this paper, and the sample is newlyweds only (first 4-5 years), a
  convenience, non-representative sample. The paper's own discussion flags that its findings "may
  not apply to other samples, i.e., more established relationships."
- Notable aside inside the discussion (their citation, not their data): "Some research suggests
  that sexual desire may decline more precipitously for women than for men (Heiman et al., 2011),
  which suggests that gender differences may emerge in more established relationships" — flagged
  by McNulty et al. themselves as a citation to outside work, not their own finding.

### van Lankveld, Dewitte, Verboon, & van Hooren (2021) — PRIMARY, VERIFIED (raw-01.txt)
*Associations of Intimacy, Partner Responsiveness, and Attachment-Related Emotional Needs With
Sexual Desire.* Frontiers in Psychology 12:665967.
- Design: ONLINE CROSS-SECTIONAL SURVEY. N=10,202 (7,701 women, 2,501 men), Dutch/Flemish
  community sample recruited via a popular magazine's website — a convenience, self-selected
  sample, not a probability sample.
- Result: "relationship duration made a significant negative contribution" to sexual desire in a
  stepwise regression: β=-0.212, t=-14.0, p<.001 (age was also significant but tiny: β=0.064).
  Confirmed again in the later structural-equation/bootstrap model: "A small but precise result was
  seen for relationship duration: longer relations showed slightly lower sexual desire."
- Can the design speak to mechanism? **No — the authors say so explicitly.** Quoting their own
  discussion: "we also acknowledge several limitations of the present study, including its
  cross-sectional design, that does not allow to investigate fluctuations in both the predictor
  variables and the criterion variable of sexual desire... The cross-sectional design of this study
  calls for cautious interpretations of the findings." Mean relationship duration in this sample
  was 6.9 years (women) / 8.7 years (men), SD ~8-10 years — i.e., people currently at longer
  duration are being COMPARED to people currently at shorter duration; this cannot distinguish a
  true within-couple decay trajectory from cohort effects, age confounds, or selection (e.g.,
  couples with chronically low desire may be more likely to have already separated and thus be
  absent from longer-duration bins — survivorship bias in the opposite direction of what naive
  duration correlations assume).
- Tier: **TIER 2** — single study, large N, but cross-sectional and a self-selected online sample.

### Klusmann (2002) — SECONDHAND (search-summary only; primary text not reached)
*Sexual Motivation and the Duration of Partnership.* Archives of Sexual Behavior 31:275-287.
- N=1,865 German students, aged 19-32, in steady partnerships; a survey design.
- Reported findings (per search-engine synthesis of the abstract/citations, not independently
  verified against the article itself): sexual activity and sexual satisfaction decline with
  partnership duration in BOTH sexes; sexual desire declines with duration ONLY in women (men's
  desire stays roughly stable); desire for tenderness declines in men and rises in women with
  duration.
- GAP: I could not reach primary text. PubMed (12049023) gives only the citation; Springer's page
  states no shareable full-text link is available; ResearchGate returned HTTP 403 to WebFetch. This
  is the single most load-bearing citation for the specific "male desire stable / female desire
  declines" sex-difference claim in the assignment brief, and it is UNVERIFIED against primary text
  here.
- Design caveat (inferred, not confirmed from primary text): this appears to be a single-wave
  survey in which duration is a between-subjects comparison across students currently at different
  partnership lengths, not a repeated-measures cohort follow-up — if so, it has the same
  duration-vs-cohort-vs-selection confound as the van Lankveld et al. cross-sectional study above.
  This inference is UNVERIFIED; I could not confirm the study's wave structure from primary text.

### Unnamed replication (Journal of Sex & Marital Therapy 38(1), 2011) — SECONDHAND
*Sexual Desire and Relationship Duration in Young Men and Women.* PubMed ID 22268980.
- N=170 undergraduates, ages 18-25, cross-sectional.
- Reported finding (search-summary only): women's sexual desire was significantly and negatively
  predicted by relationship duration after controlling for age, relationship satisfaction, and
  sexual satisfaction; men's desire was not significantly predicted by duration — i.e., a
  same-direction replication of the Klusmann sex-difference pattern in a different (US
  undergraduate) sample.
- GAP: author names are UNVERIFIED — I did not confirm authorship against a primary source and
  will not guess it. Do not cite an author name for this study without independent verification.
- Design: cross-sectional, same duration-vs-cohort/age limitation as above (age was statistically
  controlled here, which at least addresses the age confound specifically, but not cohort or
  selection).
- Tier: **TIER 2** — single study, modest N, cross-sectional, undergraduate convenience sample.

### Twenge, Sherman, & Wells (2017) and the GSS sexual-frequency-decline literature — GAP, not reached
*Declines in Sexual Frequency among American Adults, 1989-2014.* Archives of Sexual Behavior
46(8):2389-2401.
- Reported finding (search-summary only): using General Social Survey data (N=26,620,
  1989-2014), Americans had sex roughly 9 fewer times per year in the early 2010s than in the late
  1990s; the decline was concentrated among the partnered (married/cohabiting), not the
  unpartnered; an age-period-cohort decomposition attributed the decline PRIMARILY to birth
  cohort rather than to age or, by extension, relationship duration.
- I could not reach primary text or a full-text PDF (paywalled at Springer). A PDF I retrieved
  from an ASA/Socius URL that surfaced during this search turned out, on inspection, to be a
  DIFFERENT paper — South & Lei (2021), "Why Are Fewer Young Adults Having Casual Sex?," Socius
  7:1-12 — about the decline in CASUAL (non-relationship) sex among UNPARTNERED 18-23-year-olds
  (PSID-TAS data, N=945 women / 1,040 men, 2007-2017), explained mostly by declining alcohol
  consumption, rising parental coresidence, and (for men) computer gaming. This is a genuine,
  well-executed prospective panel study, but it is about unpartnered young adults' casual-sex
  frequency, NOT about desire/frequency decline WITHIN established pairs over relationship
  duration — it is off-target for claim (a) as scoped here and was not used as a raw source. Noted
  so the adjudicator does not mistake it for GSS/Twenge evidence if it resurfaces elsewhere in the
  combo run.
- If confirmed by another scout, the age-period-cohort finding is important: it suggests that a
  large share of the population-level "sex is declining" story is a COHORT (generational) effect,
  not evidence that any given pair's desire/frequency decays with ITS OWN duration. This directly
  bears on the assignment's instruction to distinguish duration effects from cohort effects.

### Basson's responsive-desire model — theory, not itself decay data
Cited only secondhand and via its appearance as background theory inside raw-01 (van Lankveld et
al. cite Basson 2000, 2002, 2003 for the circular/responsive model of female sexual response). I
did not fetch a primary Basson paper. The responsive-desire model is a clinical/theoretical
framework about the ORDER of arousal and desire (responsive vs. spontaneous), not itself a
longitudinal instrument measuring decay over duration. It is relevant as the theoretical
scaffolding cited by empirical desire researchers, not as an empirical decay finding. Tier: **TIER
3** for any decay-related claim (theory/clinical model); not evaluated here as an empirical source.

## (b) Desire declines BECAUSE OF security/familiarity — the mechanism test

**Statement:** the SPECIFIC mechanism proposed by the claim — that felt security and/or
accumulated familiarity, as distinct from mere elapsed time, causally suppresses desire.

This is the highest-value target per the assignment, and the honest answer is: **a full,
well-powered, directly-instrumented test of this specific mechanism does not appear in what I was
able to reach.** What exists is a set of partial, indirect, and in places CONTRADICTORY proxies:

### van Lankveld et al. (2021) — PRIMARY, VERIFIED (raw-01.txt) — runs counter to the claim
- This paper is the closest thing to a direct concurrent test I could verify to primary text. It
  measured "attachment-related emotional needs" (anxious and avoidant, as a proxy for attachment
  orientation, NOT a validated attachment instrument — the authors flag this as a limitation) and
  explicitly tested whether attachment MODERATES the link between intimacy and desire.
- Result: **the moderation hypothesis was REJECTED.** "The hypotheses that the link between
  intimacy and sexual desire would be moderated by attachment-related relational needs were
  rejected." Anxious attachment-related needs correlated POSITIVELY, not negatively, with desire
  (r=0.07, p<.001 — weak); avoidant attachment-related needs correlated NEGATIVELY with desire
  (r=-0.05, p<.001 — weak, and significant only in the heterosexual-women subsample). Perceived
  intimacy and perceived partner responsiveness — arguably the closest available proxies for
  "felt security" — each correlated POSITIVELY with desire at a MEDIUM effect size (both r=0.25,
  p<.001), and both independently predicted desire in the final SEM (path coefficients 0.15 and
  0.13 respectively).
- In other words: in this 10,202-person sample, MORE felt security/intimacy/responsiveness was
  associated with MORE desire, not less — the opposite direction from what the "security dampens
  desire" mechanism predicts, at least in this concurrent, self-report, non-clinical, community
  sample. This is a direct, primary-verified data point that a scout assembling this claim should
  not omit.
- Caveat: cross-sectional (see (a) above) — cannot rule out that within any given person, moving
  from LOW to HIGH security over time might still track a desire DECLINE even though, across
  people, more-secure people report more desire on average (a between-person correlation is not
  automatically the within-person trajectory). The paper cannot adjudicate this either way.
- Tier: **TIER 2** for the concurrent association; explicitly NOT a test of the decay-over-time
  mechanism (cross-sectional).

### Mizrahi et al. (2019) — SECONDHAND, not primary-verified
*When Insecurity Dampens Desire: Attachment Anxiety in Men Amplifies the Decline in Sexual Desire
during the Early Years of Romantic Relationships.* European Journal of Social Psychology (2019).
- I could NOT reach primary text — the Wiley page was not fetched, and an academia.edu mirror
  returned HTTP 403. What follows is reconstructed from (1) a PsyPost research-summary essay and
  (2) citations of the same underlying dataset ("Mizrahi et al., 2016") embedded inside the
  van Lankveld raw-01 primary text I did verify. Author list here is UNVERIFIED beyond "Mizrahi and
  colleagues" — do not cite specific co-author names from this entry.
- Reported design: TWO PROSPECTIVE LONGITUDINAL SAMPLES — dating couples (N=62, followed 8 months)
  and newlywed couples (N=175, followed 18 months).
- Reported finding: attachment ANXIETY in men moderated the TRAJECTORY (not just the level) of
  sexual desire over time — highly anxious men showed a significant decline in desire for their
  partner across the follow-up; low-anxiety men's desire did not decline. Attachment AVOIDANCE
  predicted lower INITIAL desire in both sexes (a baseline-level effect, not a trajectory effect).
  Women's desire declined regardless of attachment style (i.e., no moderation was reported for
  women).
- If accurate, this is the single closest thing in the literature to a genuine instrumented
  mechanism test for (b): it uses a longitudinal, within-person design, an individual-difference
  measure of attachment insecurity (not just presence/absence of security), and directly models
  desire CHANGE (not just desire level) as the outcome. But it tests attachment ANXIETY, which is
  conceptually closer to "insecurity/preoccupation" than to "felt security" as the claim's
  mechanism names it, and it is silent on FAMILIARITY (repeated exposure/habituation) as a
  mechanism distinct from attachment style. No exact statistics (betas, p-values, effect sizes)
  were retrievable from the sources I reached.
- GAP: primary text unreached. Flag for a follow-up scout with journal-database access
  (onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/ejsp.2567) to verify exact numbers before this is used as
  load-bearing evidence.
- Tier if the secondhand description holds up: **TIER 2** (single study, two samples, but no
  independent replication located).

### Mizrahi et al. (2016) longitudinal video-display study — PRIMARY-QUOTED VIA A SECOND PRIMARY SOURCE
Embedded, and independently confirmed, inside my verified raw-01 (van Lankveld et al. 2021)
introduction: "These associations between intimacy, attachment and sexual desire have been
investigated in a longitudinal study in which newly-wed couples were followed over a period of 8
months (Mizrahi et al., 2016). When male partners displayed sexual desire in a videotaped
interaction with their female spouse at the onset of the study, this predicted later decline in
attachment-related relationship insecurities, both of themselves and of their partner... whereas
displays of intimacy of female partners predicted later decline in their partner's
attachment-related relationship insecurities."
- This shows the REVERSE causal arrow from the claim under test: desire (displayed) → later
  reduction in insecurity, rather than security/familiarity → later reduction in desire. It is not
  itself a test of (b), but it is directly relevant counter-context: at least one longitudinal
  study finds desire display predicting MORE security later, not less desire following more
  security.
- Status: this sentence is byte-verbatim from my verified raw-01 file (i.e., I definitely read
  it, correctly, in the primary van Lankveld text), but it is van Lankveld's citation of a THIRD
  paper (Mizrahi et al. 2016) whose own primary text I have not independently read. Treat as
  "secondhand via a primary source" — reliable that van Lankveld et al. characterize it this way,
  not independently confirmed against Mizrahi et al. 2016 itself.

### van Lankveld, Jacobs, Thewissen, Dewitte, & Verboon (2018) — PARTIALLY VERIFIED
*The associations of intimacy and sexuality in daily life: Temporal dynamics and gender effects
within romantic relationships.* Journal of Social and Personal Relationships 35(4).
- Retrieved via WebFetch (a processed summary with directly quoted statistics, not an independent
  raw-HTML extraction the way raw-01/raw-02 were built — treat as PARTIALLY, not fully, verified).
- Design: experience-sampling method (ESM), N=134 participants (87 female), 10 beeps/day for 7
  consecutive days; mean relationship duration 14.6 years (already long-duration couples).
- Reported result: "A one-point increase in intimacy at time point t-1 was associated with higher
  sexual desire at time point t... about .09 [SD]"; "Daily intimacy significantly predicted sexual
  desire (B=.239, SE=.065, p<.01)." Same direction as van Lankveld 2021: more intimacy, more
  desire, not less, at the moment-to-moment level.
- Can the design speak to mechanism? No — by its own account (per the earlier WebFetch synthesis)
  it captures only WITHIN-DAY, within-person fluctuation across one week, in couples already at a
  fixed (long) average duration; it explicitly cannot test change over the course of relationship
  duration itself.
- Tier: **TIER 2**, single study, and only PARTIALLY verified here (see Confidence Notes).

### Sims & Meana (2010) — SECONDHAND (abstract-level only; full text not reached)
*Why Did Passion Wane? A Qualitative Study of Married Women's Attributions for Declines in Sexual
Desire.* Journal of Sex & Marital Therapy 36(4):360-380.
- Design: open-ended interviews, N=19 married women who reported having lost desire in their
  marriage.
- Reported themes (from the verbatim abstract, which I DID retrieve): "(a) institutionalization of
  the relationship, (b) over-familiarity, and (c) the de-sexualization of roles." The abstract
  explicitly states: "Interpersonal and intrapersonal sexual dynamics featured more prominently
  than did relationship problems in women's attributions" — i.e., in this sample, women's own
  folk-causal-attributions echoed the "familiarity" mechanism specifically named in the claim under
  test.
- GAP: full text not reached. UNLV's institutional-repository landing page
  (oasis.library.unlv.edu/psychology_fac_articles/4/) confirmed no full-text PDF is hosted there
  and pointed only to a proxy/paywall link; a related UNLV dissertation PDF
  (oasis.library.unlv.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=3776&context=rtds) returned HTTP 403.
- This is causal-ATTRIBUTION data (what respondents themselves believe caused their desire loss),
  not an instrumented test that could rule out confounds (e.g., women who already have low desire
  for other reasons may reach for "familiarity" as a culturally available explanation). It is
  essentially the general-population mirror of Perel's own clinical-impression claim — women's
  self-theories align with the clinician's, which is suggestive but not independent empirical
  confirmation of the mechanism.
- Tier: **TIER 3** — qualitative, N=19, no comparison group, self-report causal attribution.

## (c) Desire and attachment are "partially opposed systems"

**Statement:** a structural/theoretical claim that the neurobehavioral or motivational systems
underlying attachment/security and sexual desire are, to some extent, in tension with one another.

**I found no study that directly instruments this systems-level opposition claim** — e.g., no
experiment that manipulates activation of the attachment system and measures a corresponding
change in a desire-system marker (physiological, implicit, or otherwise) within the same design.
What exists is exclusively theoretical/review material:
- Diamond (2003), "What does sexual orientation orient? A biobehavioral model distinguishing
  romantic love and sexual desire," Psychological Review 110:173 — cited inside my verified raw-02
  (McNulty et al. 2016) reference list, proposing attachment/romantic-love and sexual-desire as
  partially DISSOCIABLE (not necessarily "opposed") neurobehavioral systems. Theoretical model, not
  itself decay data. Not fetched as primary text here.
- Mikulincer & Shaver's attachment-and-sexuality theoretical work, cited repeatedly as background
  theory inside raw-01, frames anxious/avoidant/secure attachment as shaping HOW people approach
  sex, not as a decay mechanism that erodes desire under security specifically.
- The empirical evidence I COULD verify to primary text runs, if anything, in the OPPOSITE
  direction from a strong "opposition" reading: in both van Lankveld et al. (2021, cross-sectional,
  N=10,202) and van Lankveld et al. (2018, daily-diary, N=134), felt intimacy/security correlates
  POSITIVELY with desire, not negatively, at both the between-person and within-day levels.
- Per the assignment's own instruction — "If nobody has instrumented it, say so plainly — that is a
  valuable finding, not a failure" — that is the finding here: **claim (c), stated as a systems-level
  opposition, appears to be theoretical scaffolding borrowed from dual-systems and attachment
  theory, not itself a measured result.** The measured associations I could locate are weakly
  positive between security-proxies and desire, not negative.

**For the maintainer, not a proposed Lab change:** if the Lab's canon ever tries to map this claim
cluster to a single tidy citation, the biggest risk is collapsing (a), (b), and (c) into one
"desire declines with familiarity, source: Klusmann/Perel" line. The primary-verified evidence here
shows (a) has real prospective support (McNulty et al.) but also real cross-sectional-only support
that cannot rule out cohort/age/selection confounds (van Lankveld et al. 2021); (b) has no primary-
verified instrumented mechanism test in hand (Mizrahi et al. 2019 is the best candidate but
unverified) and the primary-verified concurrent associations actually run positive (more intimacy/
security, more desire) rather than negative; and (c) appears to be pure theoretical scaffolding
with no direct instrumentation located at all. A canon note that says "(a) supported, prospectively,
in early marriage; (b) plausible but not directly instrumented in what was checked, and the nearest
concurrent data point runs the other way; (c) theory only" would be more defensible than a single
merged citation.
