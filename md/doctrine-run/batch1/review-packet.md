## CLUSTER 1 — "Contempt (with criticism, defensiveness, stonewalling) is the strongest behavioral predictor of pair dissolution"

Actor: established pairs. Stage: post-pairing. Mechanism: conflict style. Outcome: dissolution.
Qualifier: predictor, not cause.

**ITEM 1. An independent replication exists and it failed.**
Kim, H. K., Capaldi, D. M., & Crosby, L. (2007). "Generalizability of Gottman and Colleagues'
Affective Process Models of Couples' Relationship Outcomes." *Journal of Marriage and Family*
69(1):55–72. DOI 10.1111/j.1741-3737.2006.00343.x. PMID 17372624.
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1828692/
Oregon Social Learning Center — zero author overlap with the Gottman lab, separate sample, separate
funders, but the *same* instrument (Specific Affect Code; Gottman, McCoy & Coan 1996). Abstract,
verbatim: "The major findings of Gottman et al. failed to replicate." Specifically failing to
predict relationship status: "men's rejection of their partners' influence, the lack of men's
deescalation of partners' negative affect, and women's negative start up."
Sample: 85 married/cohabiting couples with at-risk backgrounds from the Oregon Youth Study; T1 ≈ age
21, T2 ≈ 2.5 years later; 54 intact, 31 separated.
**Stated scope limit:** Kim et al. did *not* test the discriminant function models, the contingent
positive affect path model, or the physiological soothing model. This is a failed replication of the
affective *process* models, not a head-on test of the four-horsemen divorce-prediction equation.
Population also differs (younger, at-risk, lower SES, cohabiting included, 2.5-year window).
TIER 2.

**ITEM 3. The headline accuracy figures attach to three different denominators.**
Same source as Item 2.
- **93%** — "The model predicted divorce with 93% accuracy," but that model "included marital
  satisfaction, thoughts of marital dissolution, and affective interaction in both conversations."
  Not behavior alone.

**ITEM 10. Prediction versus postdiction.**
Heyman, R. E., & Slep, A. M. S. (2001). "The Hazards of Predicting Divorce Without Crossvalidation."
*Journal of Marriage and Family* 63(2):473–479. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1622921/
Verbatim: "the analyst asks the software to *reconstruct,* rather than predict, because the computer
develops an equation to optimally reconstruct an already-known group status. This is not a trivial,
semantic distinction." On the standard: "crossvalidation only provides evidence of the accuracy of a
predictive equation when the original weights and cut-points are used; developing another 'highly
predictive' equation with new weights and cut-points is not sufficient."
Field census from the same paper: "only 15 published studies have predicted *who* will get
divorced... accuracy... ranging from 67% to 95%... following couples for 2–15 years. Sample sizes
ranged from 54 to 286." And: "No published study predicting divorce with general population couples
has done this to date," with one carve-out for Crane et al. 1995 (a therapy sample whose scale asked
about steps already taken toward divorce).
TIER 2 (the underlying statistical principle is textbook).

**ITEM 11. A worked demonstration: 90% in-sample accuracy becomes 21% positive predictive value.**
Same source as Item 10. Data: 1985 National Family Violence Survey, n = 6,002; 176 divorced plus 176
highest- and 176 lowest-disagreement cases = 528, split into two independent subsamples of 88
divorced and 176 married each. Backward stepwise logistic regression; ROC cut-point 0.222157493.
- **Development sample:** 34 TP / 18 FP / 3 FN / 149 TN — "Percent correct: 89.71%; sensitivity:
  91.89%; specificity: 89.22%; PVpos: 65.38%; PVneg: 98.03%."
- **Cross-validation sample:** 17 TP / 42 FP / 20 FN / 123 TN — "Percent correct: 69.31%;
  sensitivity: 45.95%; specificity: 74.55%; PVpos: 28.81%; PVneg: 86.01%."
- **Re-weighted to a 16% base rate** (Clarke 1995): "PVpos: 20.99%." Summary sentence: "An equation
  with an initial overall accuracy of 90% ended up with a positive predictive value of 21%."
- **PRESERVED SOURCE DISCREPANCY, added at review.** The design description above is quoted faithfully
  from the archived source, which states the split verbatim: "We randomly split the 176 divorced
  participants and the 352 married or cohabiting participants into two subsamples, each with 88
  divorced and 176 married or living together participants." But the paper's own confusion matrices
  classify 37 divorced cases per subsample (34+3 development; 17+20 crossvalidation), not 88, against
  167 and 165 married rather than 176. All ten derived statistics reproduce exactly from the matrices,
  so the matrices are internally sound; the unexplained reduction — most plausibly listwise deletion on
  the regression's predictors, which the text does not state — is **the source's**, not this packet's.
  Recorded here rather than reconciled, alongside the sensitivity/specificity defect below.
- Applied to Gottman et al. 1998, assuming 80% sensitivity and 80% specificity at 16% prevalence:
  "The positive predictive value for Gottman et al.'s equation would be 43%... one would be wrong
  more than half of the time when one told couples 'you are likely to get divorced.'"
**Stated limit, in the authors' own words:** "the archival data set used was cross-sectional, unlike
the prospective data of Gottman and colleagues." This demonstrates a hazard; it is not a re-analysis
of Gottman's data.
A textual defect is preserved in the archived source: "sensitivity dropped 45% and its sensitivity
dropped 15%" — the second instance evidently should read *specificity*.

## CLUSTER 2 — "Erotic desire decays under security and familiarity within established pairs unless actively renewed; desire and attachment are partially opposed systems"

Stage: post-pairing. Timeframe: years. Qualifier: decay tendency, not law. Split into three
sub-claims, kept separate: **(a)** desire/frequency/satisfaction decline with duration; **(b)**
they decline *because of* security/familiarity; **(c)** desire and attachment are partially opposed
systems.

**ITEM 14. (a) The strongest design: prospective, multi-wave, and it finds decline.**
McNulty, J. K., Wenner, C. A., & Fisher, T. D. (2016). "Longitudinal Associations among Relationship
Satisfaction, Sexual Satisfaction, and Frequency of Sex in Early Marriage." *Archives of Sexual
Behavior* 45(1):85–97. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4472635/
Two prospective longitudinal studies of newlyweds; 8 waves of satisfaction and 7 of frequency at 6–8
month intervals, spanning ~4–4.5 years. Study 1 N = 72 couples (north-central Ohio); Study 2 N = 135
couples (eastern Tennessee); combined N = 207. Both convenience samples, "neither representative of
the community from which the couples were drawn."
Growth-curve estimates (HLM, df = 206): marital satisfaction Time b = −0.44, p < .001, r = .48;
sexual satisfaction Time b = −1.27, p < .001, r = .36; frequency of sex Time b = −3.96, p < .001,
r = .29. Significant positive Time×Time terms (marital b = 0.15, r = .43; sexual satisfaction
b = 0.43, r = .35; frequency b = 0.83, r = .18) make the decline decelerating rather than linear.
**What it cannot do:** no security or familiarity construct is in the model, so it cannot speak to
*why*. The paper's own discussion flags that findings "may not apply to other samples, i.e., more
established relationships."
**TIER 2** — single paper, no independent replication, newlyweds only; but genuinely prospective,
multi-wave and attrition-controlled across two same-lab samples (N = 207 couples), which makes it the
strongest design in this cluster even at TIER 2. An earlier draft labelled this "TIER 1-adjacent,"
which is not a rung in this packet's own scheme and claimed better-than-TIER-2 standing without
meeting the stated TIER 1 bar.

**ITEM 15. (a) A large cross-sectional association, with the authors' own causal disclaimer.**
van Lankveld, J., Dewitte, M., Verboon, P., & van Hooren, S. (2021). "Associations of Intimacy,
Partner Responsiveness, and Attachment-Related Emotional Needs With Sexual Desire." *Frontiers in
Psychology* 12:665967. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8255964/
Online cross-sectional survey, N = 10,202 (7,701 women, 2,501 men), Dutch/Flemish community sample
recruited via a magazine website — self-selected, not a probability sample. "Relationship duration
made a significant negative contribution" to desire: β = −0.212, t = −14.0, p < .001 (age also
significant but small, β = 0.064).
**Authors' own limitation, verbatim:** "we also acknowledge several limitations of the present study,
including its cross-sectional design, that does not allow to investigate fluctuations in both the
predictor variables and the criterion variable of sexual desire... The cross-sectional design of this
study calls for cautious interpretations of the findings."
Mean duration 6.9 years (women) / 8.7 years (men). Longer-duration people are being compared with
shorter-duration people, so duration cannot be separated from cohort, age, or selection — including
survivorship in the opposite direction from what naive duration correlations assume.
TIER 2.

**ITEM 16. (b) The mechanism test — the load-bearing half — was not found.**
A well-powered, directly-instrumented test of whether felt security or accumulated familiarity, as
distinct from elapsed time, causally suppresses desire does not appear anywhere in what was reached.
Recorded as an absence, not as a negative result.

**ITEM 17. (b) The nearest primary-verified evidence runs in the OPPOSITE direction.**
Same source as Item 15. It measured attachment-related emotional needs (anxious and avoidant — a
proxy, and the authors flag that it is not a validated attachment instrument) and explicitly tested
whether attachment moderates the intimacy–desire link.
**The moderation hypothesis was rejected**, verbatim: "The hypotheses that the link between intimacy
and sexual desire would be moderated by attachment-related relational needs were rejected."
Anxious attachment-related needs correlated *positively* with desire (r = 0.07, p < .001, weak);
avoidant needs negatively (r = −0.05, p < .001, weak). Both are full-sample estimates.
**CORRECTED at review** — an earlier draft of this item attached a subsample caveat to the avoidant
correlation and located it in heterosexual women. Re-checked against the archived source text, the
caveat belongs to the *anxious* correlation and runs the other way: it "was positive and significant
in the full sample and all subsamples, but not significant in the subsample of non-heterosexual men."
Perceived intimacy and perceived partner responsiveness — the closest available proxies for felt
security — each "correlated positively and significantly with sexual desire (both r = 0.25,
p < .001)," and each independently predicted desire in the final SEM (paths 0.15 and 0.13). The
source assigns these coefficients no magnitude adjective; none is supplied here.
**Caveat recorded:** a between-person correlation is not automatically the within-person trajectory;
this cross-sectional design cannot adjudicate either way.
TIER 2 for the concurrent association; explicitly not a test of decay over time.

**ITEM 18. (b) The best candidate mechanism test was not reached.**
Mizrahi et al. (2019). "When Insecurity Dampens Desire: Attachment Anxiety in Men Amplifies the
Decline in Sexual Desire during the Early Years of Romantic Relationships." *European Journal of
Social Psychology*. DOI 10.1002/ejsp.2567.
**GAP.** Wiley not fetched; academia.edu mirror returned HTTP 403. Reconstructed from a PsyPost
research summary plus citations of the same underlying dataset inside the verified van Lankveld text.
**Author list UNVERIFIED beyond "Mizrahi and colleagues" — do not cite specific co-author names.**
Reported design: two prospective samples — dating couples N = 62 followed 8 months, newlyweds N = 175
followed 18 months. Reported finding: attachment *anxiety* in men moderated the desire *trajectory*;
highly anxious men declined, low-anxiety men did not; avoidance predicted lower *initial* desire in
both sexes; women's desire declined regardless of attachment style.
**No exact statistics were retrievable.** Note also that it tests attachment anxiety — conceptually
closer to insecurity than to *felt security* as the claim's mechanism names it — and is silent on
familiarity/habituation as a distinct mechanism.
**TIER 3 as sourced** — reached only through a press summary and a citation inside another paper, with
no statistics and an unverified author list. An earlier draft graded this "TIER 2 if the secondhand
description holds," which grades a hypothetical; tier tracks how a source was actually reached. Were
the primary read and the description confirmed, it would rate TIER 2.

**ITEM 19. (b) One longitudinal finding runs the causal arrow backwards.**
Quoted byte-verbatim from the verified van Lankveld text (Item 15), which is citing Mizrahi et al.
(2016): "When male partners displayed sexual desire in a videotaped interaction with their female
spouse at the onset of the study, this predicted later decline in attachment-related relationship
insecurities, both of themselves and of their partner... whereas displays of intimacy of female
partners predicted later decline in their partner's attachment-related relationship insecurities."
That is desire → later *reduced* insecurity, rather than security → later reduced desire.
**Status:** reliable that van Lankveld et al. characterise it this way; Mizrahi et al. 2016 itself
was not independently read.

**ITEM 20. (b) The one source naming familiarity is attribution data, not instrumentation.**
Sims, K. E., & Meana, M. (2010). "Why Did Passion Wane? A Qualitative Study of Married Women's
Attributions for Declines in Sexual Desire." *Journal of Sex & Marital Therapy* 36(4):360–380.
Open-ended interviews, N = 19 married women who reported losing desire. Themes, from the verbatim
abstract that *was* retrieved: "(a) institutionalization of the relationship, (b) over-familiarity,
and (c) the de-sexualization of roles." Also: "Interpersonal and intrapersonal sexual dynamics
featured more prominently than did relationship problems in women's attributions."
**GAP:** full text not reached (UNLV repository hosts no PDF; a related dissertation PDF returned

not an instrumented test that could rule out confounds.
TIER 3.

**ITEM 22. (c) The systems-opposition claim has no located instrumentation at all.**
No study was found that manipulates attachment-system activation and measures a corresponding change
in a desire-system marker within one design. What exists is theoretical: Diamond (2003),
*Psychological Review* 110:173, proposing attachment/romantic-love and sexual desire as partially
*dissociable* — not necessarily opposed — neurobehavioral systems (cited inside the verified McNulty
reference list, not fetched).
An earlier draft also characterised "Mikulincer & Shaver's attachment-and-sexuality work" as framing
attachment styles as shaping *how* people approach sex rather than as a decay mechanism. **That
characterisation carried no year, venue, or locator and is withdrawn** rather than repaired: the names
appear only as background citations inside the archived van Lankveld text, and no work of theirs was
reached in this batch.
The measured associations that could be verified run *positive* between security proxies and desire, at
both between-person (ITEM 17) and within-day levels. **Consistency note:** an earlier draft called the
same coefficients "weakly positive" here and "medium effect size" at ITEM 17; the source supplies no
magnitude adjective, so both are withdrawn and the coefficients stand as r = 0.25, p < .001. The within-day source is van
Lankveld, Jacobs, Thewissen, Dewitte & Verboon (2018), *Journal of Social and Personal
Relationships* 35(4), experience sampling, N = 134, 10 beeps/day for 7 days, mean duration 14.6
years: "Daily intimacy significantly predicted sexual desire (B=.239, SE=.065, p<.01)."
**Provenance note:** the 2018 figures were reached via fetch-tool summarisation rather than raw-text
extraction — **partially verified**, treat statistics as probably but not certainly exact.
Claim (c) as sourced here: TIER 3, theoretical scaffolding.

**ITEM 23. A recorded dead end, so it is not re-walked.**

## CLUSTER 3 — "Being higher mate-value than one's partner degrades satisfaction and raises infidelity intent, mainly when attractive alternatives are visibly available"

Stage: post-pairing. Qualifier: conditional on alternatives visibility. Split into **(a)** the main
effect and **(b)** the conditional.

**ITEM 24. (a) A main effect on satisfaction — but for a pool-comparison construct, not for
self-versus-partner mate value discrepancy.**
**SCOPE CORRECTED at review.** The cluster claim is about being higher mate value than *one's own
partner*. The construct headlined in this item is a *pool* comparison, built almost identically to the
"availability of better alternatives" term whose construct mismatch is flagged at ITEM 27. It does not
test the cluster claim's main effect; **ITEM 25 is the item that does.** An earlier draft presented
this as "the main effect on satisfaction" without that distinction.
Conroy-Beam, D., Goetz, C. D., & Buss, D. M. (2016). "What predicts romantic relationship
satisfaction and mate retention intensity: mate preference fulfillment or mate value discrepancies?"
*Evolution and Human Behavior* 37(6):440–448. DOI 10.1016/j.evolhumbehav.2016.04.003.
Partner-potential mate value discrepancy — how much better other people's actual partners fulfil a
person's stated preferences than their own partner does — negatively predicts satisfaction. Study 1,
N = 259 (MTurk, heterosexual, ongoing long-term relationships), forced-entry regression β = −.53,
p < .001; zero-order r = −.50. Study 2, N = 300, zero-order r = −.39. Study 3, N = 301.
All three studies cross-sectional, single-timepoint online survey.
TIER 2 — three internal replications, but same three authors, same lab, "identical posting" on
MTurk across studies, no independent replication.

**ITEM 25. (a) A weak, marginal, superseded main effect.**
Same source. Partner-self mate value discrepancy (partner rated higher than self) positively predicts
satisfaction: Study 1 β = .11, **p = .05 (marginal)**. The authors' own stepwise model selection
dropped preference fulfilment and retained the discrepancy *interaction*, so this simple main effect
is not the paper's best-supported result.
TIER 2, marginal.

**ITEM 26. (a) The other tradition's main effect, with an unreported N.**
Miller, R. S. (1997). *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 73(4):758–766, as restated by
the author in his 2007 Sydney Symposium draft chapter
(https://sydneysymposium.unsw.edu.au/2007/Chapters/MillerSSSP07.pdf).
Attention to alternatives correlates negatively with satisfaction r = −.58, investment r = −.52,
commitment r = −.61, love r = −.59, liking r = −.39. **The N for this sub-sample is not stated in
the chapter text and is UNVERIFIED.**
Longitudinal extension, Simeon & Miller (2005), 3-month cross-lagged panel, N = 167:
satisfaction(T1)→attentiveness(T2) r = −.47; attentiveness(T1)→satisfaction(T2) r = −.38, i.e.
bidirectional.
The 1997 finding: TIER 2. The cross-lag figures: **TIER 3** — an unpublished conference
presentation reported secondhand inside the author's own later chapter.

**ITEM 27. (b) The interaction test exists.**
Same source as Item 24, full text archived. Relative mate value discrepancy and the availability of
better alternatives interact: satisfaction declines with increasing alternative availability, but
only for people higher in mate value than their partner; people whose partner is higher in mate
value stay satisfied regardless of alternatives.
- Study 1, N = 259: interaction β = .21, p < .001; full model R² = 31.34%.
- Study 2, N = 300: interaction β = .28, p < .001; path model shows the interaction mediating through
  satisfaction to *increased* mate-retention behaviour (indirect path p = .01) and through trust to
  *decreased* mate-retention behaviour (indirect path p = .001).
- Study 3, N = 301 (same-scale replication addressing a measurement-scale confound in Studies 1–2):
  interaction β = −.20, p < .001. The sign is opposite to Studies 1–2. **ATTRIBUTION CORRECTED at
  review:** the reconciliation offered for that reversal — differing coding conventions between the
  forced-entry and stepwise specifications, with the plotted interaction directionally identical — is
  the **research scout's inference, not the paper's argument**. Re-checked against the archived source,
  the paper says only that "Study 3 replicated the relationship between mate value discrepancies and
  relationship satisfaction found in Studies 1 and 2" and never addresses a sign reversal. The paper
  does separately volunteer that a *different* Study 3 result "could indicate that Study 3's result was
  a type I error," which concerns preference fulfilment rather than the interaction.
- **No study in this set measured infidelity or infidelity intent.** The dependent variables are
  relationship satisfaction (QMI) and mate-retention effort.
**CRITICAL CONSTRUCT MISMATCH:** the "alternatives" term is not a measure of subjective visibility
or awareness. It is an objectively computed statistic — the proportion of *other survey respondents'*
actual partners, people the focal participant never met or saw, who would fulfil the participant's
stated preferences better than their own partner does. The authors flag the underlying limitation:
"the participants in our samples were drawn from a national population and are virtually guaranteed
to not know one another." This tests discrepancy × *statistical availability in a hypothetical pool*,
not discrepancy × *perceived/visible* alternatives.
TIER 2.

**ITEM 28. (b) One dataset, cited twice, reads as two supporting sources.**
Buss, D. M., Goetz, C., Duntley, J. D., Asao, K., & Conroy-Beam, D. (2017). "The mate switching
hypothesis." *Personality and Individual Differences* 104:143–149.
It states as established fact: "Individuals show decreased relationship satisfaction when they
perceive they are higher in mate value than their romantic partner, but only if there exist highly
desirable alternatives in the mating pool (Conroy-Beam et al., in press)." That citation is the
identical dataset as Item 27 — "Conroy-Beam et al., in press" *is* the 2016 EHB paper.
Conroy-Beam and Goetz appear on both; Buss is senior author on both; both are UT Austin Buss-lab
output; the windows overlap (empirical paper received June 2015, revised April 2016; review received
Feb 2016, accepted July 2016). **No other empirical citation for this specific conditional appears
anywhere in the review paper, which was read in full.**

**ITEM 29. (b) The closest true "visibility" analog, from the independent tradition.**
Miller's chapter (Item 26), citing Simeon & Miller (2005): attention to alternatives *mediates* the
effect of alternative quality on subsequent commitment; the erosion is stronger when alternatives are
actually attended to. N = 167, Sobel's test t(167) = −7.58, p < .001, partial mediation.
Visibility/attention is the literal mechanism tested — but the outcome is **commitment**, not
satisfaction or infidelity intent; it is not framed in mate-value-discrepancy terms at all; and the
mediation statistic comes from an unpublished conference paper.
TIER 3 for the mediation figure; TIER 2 for the surrounding pattern.

**ITEM 30. (b) A finding that would work against the claim.**
Johnson, D. J., & Rusbult, C. E. (1989). More-committed people devalue attractive alternatives more
strongly, most of all under high threat — when personally evaluating an extremely attractive, highly
salient alternative in a live computer-dating paradigm. That is an inverse-direction conditional:
commitment causes people to perceptually neutralise visible alternatives, a self-protective loop
working against the claim rather than for it.
**GAP — and this item fails the citation floor, flagged at review.** The citation carries authors and a
year only: **no journal, volume, pages, DOI, or URL**. Its retrieval basis was a WebSearch synthesis
plus Miller's secondhand citations — a synthesis is not a named source. Sample sizes and statistics for
its three sub-studies are **UNVERIFIED**, and so, less obviously, are the paradigm description and the
moderation pattern above, which an earlier draft presented without a flag.
The claim that "Rusbult was at the University of Kentucky at the time, making this the one clearly
independent data point in this tradition" is **withdrawn as an uncited biographical assertion**. It was
carrying the item's entire independence conclusion, and lineage independence is evidenced explicitly
everywhere else in this packet (ITEMs 9, 12, 28) rather than asserted.
TIER 3 as sourced. Retained despite the citation failure because it is a *disconfirming* finding, and
dropping it would bias the cluster; it must be re-cited before any use.

**ITEM 31. (b) The only source combining both predictors with an infidelity outcome was not reached.**
"Pathways to online infidelity: the roles of perceived online dating success, perceived availability
of alternative partners, and mate value discrepancy" (ResearchGate ID 374848897). Reported: a mediation
model, N = 338, cross-sectional dating-app sample.
**GAP — HTTP 403.** Authors, journal, and year unconfirmed. Critically, whether the two predictors
enter as serial mediators, parallel mediators, or a multiplicative interaction could **not** be
confirmed; the search paraphrase reads as parallel mediation. **Corrected at review:** an earlier draft
asserted "serial mediation" and "parallel mediation" about the same paper four lines apart — different
models, only one of which can be what the paraphrase said.
TIER 3 as sourced.

**ITEM 32. (a) An infidelity claim with no recoverable citation.**
"Mate value both positively and negatively predicts intentions to commit an infidelity"
(ScienceDirect record S0191886916308686; journal inferred from the ISSN prefix to be *Personality and
Individual Differences* but **not confirmed**). Reported: a person's overall mate value negatively
predicts their own infidelity intention, with four sub-components each uniquely predicting.
**GAP — HTTP 403/402. No author names, year, or N recovered.** Note this is about *own absolute*
mate value, not relative discrepancy, and there is no indication an alternatives moderator was
tested.
TIER 3.

**ITEM 33. A gendered infidelity claim, reached only through a review.**
Relationship dissatisfaction is among the most powerful predictors of women's actual infidelity but
not men's. Advanced by Buss et al. (2017) (Item 28), citing Glass & Wright (1985, 1992) as the data
source. **Glass & Wright not fetched — GAP, secondhand via the review.**
TIER 3 as sourced.

**ITEM 34. No formal interaction with an infidelity outcome was found.**
Item 27 tests the interaction but with satisfaction and mate-retention effort as outcomes. Item 31
puts both predictors in one model with an infidelity-adjacent outcome but appears — unconfirmed — to
use parallel mediation rather than a multiplicative term. No study was located testing (relative mate
value discrepancy) × (visibility/availability of alternatives) predicting infidelity or infidelity
intent as a formal interaction. **Recorded as the honest state of what was reachable, explicitly not
as proof of non-existence.**

**ITEM 35. The two traditions never unify their vocabulary.**
The evolutionary-psychology side uses "mate value discrepancy" and "partner-potential mate value
discrepancy" (an objective computed pool measure). The interdependence/investment-model side
(Thibaut & Kelley → Rusbult → Johnson & Rusbult → Miller) uses "comparison level for alternatives"
(CLalt), "quality of alternatives" (a formal term in Rusbult's model), and "attention to
alternatives" (Miller's subjective awareness/interest/distraction construct).
Neither is a clean match for "visibility": the evolutionary-psych side has an objective pool variable
but no subjective visibility variable; the investment-model side has subjective attention and
perceived quality but does not frame the moderator in terms of the chooser's own relative mate value.
**No paper unifying them was found.**
For scale on the investment-model main effect: Tran, P., Judge, M., & Kashima, Y. (2019),
"Commitment in relationships: An updated meta-analysis of the Investment Model," *Personal
Relationships* — reported 202 independent samples, 50,427 participants, 1980–2016, aggregate
r = −.43 between quality of alternatives and commitment. **GAP — Wiley HTTP 402; volume, pages and DOI
not recorded; figures are as reported by search engines summarising the abstract, not independently
verified. TIER 3 as sourced** — an earlier draft described it in evidential terms ("broad multi-lab
support") while carrying no tier at all. Were the primary read, the underlying meta-analysis would rate
TIER 1. *If confirmed*, it would be broad multi-lab support for an unmoderated main effect on
commitment, not for any interaction with relative mate value.
